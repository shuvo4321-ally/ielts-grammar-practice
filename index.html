Part 1 · How grammar is marked

Read this once. It decides how you use everything after it.

One quarter of your Writing and Speaking score is grammar. The examiner looks at two things, and they pull in opposite directions:

  • Range: do you use a variety of sentence types (complex sentences, passives, conditionals, relative and participle clauses)?
  • Accuracy: how many of your sentences are completely free of errors?

Most band-7 candidates lose marks on accuracy, not range. The fastest way to band 8 is not new structures. It is the same structures with fewer mistakes.

BandWhat the grammar looks like (in plain words)
Band 6A mix of simple and complex sentences, but complex ones often contain errors. Errors rarely stop the reader understanding.
Band 7A variety of complex structures. Many sentences are error-free. A few errors keep coming back (articles, agreement, punctuation).
Band 8A wide range of structures. Most sentences are completely error-free. Only occasional slips.
Band 9A full range used naturally and flexibly. Errors are rare and look like slips, not gaps in knowledge.

A plain-English summary of the public band descriptors for grammatical range and accuracy, not the official wording.

Three rules for band 8+
  1. Accuracy first. A short sentence that is correct beats a long one with an error. Every error-free sentence counts in your favour.
  2. Range from a handful of structures. Relative clauses, conditionals, the passive, participle clauses and noun phrases (Units 3, 12, 13, 18, 19) give you all the range you need for band 8.
  3. Know your own top five errors. Do the Error Hunt (Part 3). Whatever you miss there is what you check for in the last three minutes of the test.

How to use this book

  • Each unit: the rule → where it fits in the test → right and wrong pairs → band 7 vs band 9 → four quick questions.
  • Answer each question, then open Show answer underneath. It tells you if you were right and why.
  • In Spot the error questions, tap the word that is wrong.
  • Your answers are saved in this browser only.

UNIT 1Task 1 · charts and tables
Tense follows the dates on the chart
The rule

Look at the time axis before you write a single verb. The dates choose the tense, not you.

  • Dates in the past (1990–2010): past simple. Sales rose, peaked, fell.
  • No dates, or a table of current facts: present simple. Men spend more time on sport.
  • Dates in the future (2030, 2050): projection language. is expected to, is projected to, is predicted to, will.
  • Past dates that run into future dates: switch tense at the point where the data becomes a forecast.

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
Line graph 1990–2020past simpleThe number of visitors doubled between 1990 and 2005.
Table with no yearpresent simpleJapan has the highest proportion of elderly people.
Forecast to 2050is expected to / is projected toThe figure is projected to reach 40% by 2050.
Overview sentencepast simple or present, matching the dataOverall, car ownership increased in every country.
Right and wrong

✗ In 2005, the number of tourists has reached 2 million.

✓ In 2005, the number of tourists reached 2 million.

A finished past date (in 2005) never takes the present perfect.

✗ By 2040, the price rises to $8.

✓ By 2040, the price is expected to rise to $8.

A forecast is not a fact. Present simple states it as certain, which misreports the chart.

✗ The table shows that in 2015 Germany produces the most.

✓ The table shows that in 2015 Germany produced the most.

"The table shows" stays present (the table exists now), but the data verb follows the date.

Band 7 → band 9

BAND 7 The number of cars will be increasing to 50 million in 2030.

BAND 9 The number of cars is projected to climb to around 50 million by 2030.

Band 9 uses a projection verb (is projected to), the right preposition for a deadline (by) and an approximation word.

Check yourself

1Between 1995 and 2005, coal consumption ______ by a third.
Show answer

fell

Why
A closed period in the past with both dates given: past simple.

2According to the forecast, the population of the city ______ 12 million by 2050.
Show answer

is predicted to reach

Why
2050 is a forecast, so it needs projection language. Reaches states it as a fact.

3The pie chart shows how an average household (spend) its money today.
Show answer

spends

Why
No past date and the word today: present simple, third person -s.

4Tap the part that is wrong.
Show answer

Wrong: have peaked

✓ The graph shows that sales peaked at 800 units in 2012 before declining.

Why
In 2012 is a finished past time. Present perfect cannot sit with it.

Remember

Read the dates first. Past dates: past simple. No dates: present simple. Future dates: is expected / projected to.

UNIT 2Task 1 + Task 2 + Speaking
Past simple or present perfect?
The rule

Both talk about the past. The difference is whether the time is finished.

  • Past simple: a finished time that is named or understood: in 2010, last year, when I was a child, during the 1990s.
  • Present perfect: a period that runs up to now: since 2010, over the last decade, in recent years, so far, recently.
  • Duration up to now: for / since with the present perfect. Prices have risen for five years / since 2019.

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
Task 1, closed periodpast simpleUnemployment fell in 2018.
Task 2, change up to nowpresent perfectOnline learning has become far more common in recent years.
Speaking, life experiencepresent perfectI've been to Chittagong twice.
Speaking, a storypast simpleI went there last summer and stayed a week.
Right and wrong

✗ In recent years, many people moved to cities.

✓ In recent years, many people have moved to cities.

In recent years reaches up to now, so it takes the present perfect.

✗ I have visited London in 2019.

✓ I visited London in 2019.

A finished date (in 2019) needs past simple.

✗ I live here since 2015.

✓ I've lived (or 've been living) here since 2015.

Since + a starting point means a period up to now: present perfect, never present simple.

Band 7 → band 9

BAND 7 Nowadays technology changed our life a lot.

BAND 9 Over the past two decades, technology has transformed the way people work and communicate.

A time frame that reaches now (over the past two decades) with the present perfect, and a precise verb instead of changed a lot.

Check yourself

1Since the 1980s, the gap between rich and poor ______ steadily.
Show answer

has widened

Why
Since the 1980s runs up to now: present perfect.

2The bridge ______ in 1998 and cut journey times by half.
Show answer

was opened

Why
A finished date (1998): past simple. Passive, because the bridge did not open itself.

3I (know) my best friend for about ten years now.
Show answer

have knownalso accepted: 've known, ’ve known

Why
Know is a state verb, so no -ing; duration up to now with for: present perfect.

4Tap the part that is wrong.
Show answer

Wrong: have introduced

✓ Many governments introduced sugar taxes in 2016.

Why
In 2016 is a finished time. Either drop the date or use past simple.

Remember

A named finished time takes past simple. Since, for, recently, in recent years, so far take the present perfect.

UNIT 3Task 1 · process diagrams
The passive in process descriptions
The rule

In a process, what matters is what happens to the material, not who does it. So the passive is the normal choice.

  • Form: is / are + past participle. The leaves are dried. The mixture is then heated.
  • Leave out by + doer unless the doer is important: The bottles are collected by lorries is fine; by workers usually adds nothing.
  • Natural processes (life cycles) use the active: The eggs hatch. The larva grows.
  • Some verbs are never passive: consist of, happen, occur, become, take place.

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
Manufacturing, recycling, productionpresent simple passiveThe glass is crushed and then melted.
Natural cyclepresent simple activeThe frog lays its eggs in water.
Overview of a processactive, with a countThe process consists of six stages.
Sequencingpassive + sequencersOnce it has been cleaned, the wool is spun into yarn.
Right and wrong

✗ The process is consisted of five stages.

✓ The process consists of five stages.

Consist of is never passive.

✗ Then workers heat the sand to 1,500°C.

✓ The sand is then heated to 1,500°C.

The workers are obvious. The passive keeps the focus on the material.

✗ The caterpillar is turned into a butterfly.

✓ The caterpillar turns into a butterfly.

Nobody turns it. Natural changes are active.

Band 7 → band 9

BAND 7 First they pick the tea and then they dry it and after that they put it in bags.

BAND 9 Once the leaves have been picked, they are dried for several hours before being packed into bags.

Passive throughout, a time clause (once…) and a passive -ing form (before being packed) instead of a chain of and then.

Check yourself

1At the next stage, the clay ______ into bricks.
Show answer

is shaped

Why
The clay does not shape itself: present simple passive.

2The whole cycle ______ about three weeks.
Show answer

takes

Why
Take meaning last is active; a general fact about the cycle: present simple.

3After the cans (collect), they are taken to a recycling plant.
Show answer

are collectedalso accepted: have been collected

Why
Passive, because the cans are collected by someone. Present simple or present perfect passive both work after after.

4Tap the part that is wrong.
Show answer

Wrong: is evaporated

✓ The water is boiled and then it evaporates into steam.

Why
Evaporating happens by itself. It is an intransitive verb, so it has no passive.

Remember

Things done to the material: passive. Things that happen by themselves: active. Consist of and happen: never passive.

UNIT 4Task 1 · charts
Verbs and nouns of change, and their prepositions
The rule

One small word changes the meaning of the whole sentence:

  • rose to 20%: the end point. rose by 5%: the size of the change. rose from 15% to 20%: start and end.
  • As a noun: a rise in + the thing, a rise of + the amount. a rise of 5% in sales.
  • stood at / peaked at / bottomed out at + a figure. levelled off at + a figure.
  • Verb pairs: rise / raise: prices rise (no object); the government raises taxes (object).

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
Size of changebyExports grew by 12%.
End pointtoExports grew to $40bn.
Starting figurestood at / began atIn 2000 the rate stood at 7%.
Noun phrasea + adjective + noun + inThere was a sharp fall in imports.
Right and wrong

✗ Sales increased with 10%.

✓ Sales increased by 10%.

The amount of change takes by.

✗ There was a significant increase of the number of students.

✓ There was a significant increase in the number of students.

in before the thing that changed; of only before an amount.

✗ The price was raised to $5 in 2010.

✓ The price rose to $5 in 2010.

Raised needs someone doing it. For a chart, use rose.

Band 7 → band 9

BAND 7 The number went up and then it went down and then it was the same.

BAND 9 The figure climbed to a peak of 60 in 2008, dropped sharply over the following two years and then levelled off at around 35.

Exact prepositions (to a peak of, at around), varied verbs and one clear time line.

Check yourself

1Rice exports fell ______ 4 million tonnes, reaching a low of 2 million.
Show answer

by

Why
4 million is the size of the fall (6 → 2), so by.

2There was a dramatic rise ______ the cost of housing.
Show answer

in

Why
A rise in + the thing that rose.

3The unemployment rate peaked 11% in 2012.
Show answer

at

Why
Peak at, stand at, level off at + the figure.

4Tap the part that is wrong.
Show answer

Wrong: rose

✓ The government raised the minimum wage twice in five years.

Why
With an object (the minimum wage), the verb is raise. Rise never takes an object.

Remember

to = where it ended. by = how much it moved. a rise in the thing, a rise of the amount.

UNIT 5Task 1 · comparisons
Comparing figures accurately
The rule
  • One syllable: -er / -est (higher, the lowest). Two or more: more / most (more popular). Never both: more higher.
  • Multiples: twice as many as / three times as high as, or twice the number of.
  • Size of a difference: slightly / marginally / considerably / significantly / far + comparative. Not very higher.
  • Uncountable: much / far more money. Countable: many / far more people. Fewer for countables, less for uncountables.
  • Equality: the same as, as … as, and for a near match almost as … as.

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
Two figures, big gapfar / considerably + comparativeWomen's wages were considerably lower than men's.
A multipletwice / three times as … asSpain received twice as many visitors as Greece.
Top or bottomthe + superlativeOil was the most widely used fuel.
Similar figures(almost) as … asRail was almost as popular as bus.
Right and wrong

✗ The figure for Canada was more higher than Mexico.

✓ The figure for Canada was higher than that of Mexico.

No double comparative, and compare figure with figure (that of), not figure with country.

✗ Japan had two times more cars than Korea.

✓ Japan had twice as many cars as Korea.

Twice as many … as is the accurate pattern for multiples.

✗ Less people travelled by train.

✓ Fewer people travelled by train.

People are countable: fewer.

Band 7 → band 9

BAND 7 The USA was very higher than China and China was less than India.

BAND 9 The figure for the USA was considerably higher than that for China, which in turn was slightly below India's.

Grading words (considerably, slightly), that for to compare like with like, and a non-defining clause to chain three items.

Check yourself

1Coal accounted for ______ electricity as gas did.
Show answer

twice as much

Why
Electricity is uncountable: twice as much … as.

2Consumption in 2020 was ______ than in 2010.
Show answer

much higher

Why
Comparatives are graded with much, far, slightly, considerably, never very.

3There were (few) accidents in 2019 than in any other year.
Show answer

fewer

Why
Accidents are countable: fewer, not less.

4Tap the part that is wrong.
Show answer

Wrong: Manchester

✓ The average salary in London was higher than that in Manchester.

Why
Compare salary with salary: than that in Manchester (or than in Manchester), not salary with a city.

Remember

Never more + -er or very + comparative. Compare like with like: that of / those of.

UNIT 6Task 1 · data nouns
The number of, a number of, and percentages
The rule

Agreement with data words is the most common Task 1 grammar error. The verb agrees with the head noun:

  • The number of students is rising (head = number, singular).
  • A number of students are unhappy (means several, plural).
  • The percentage / proportion / amount of … is / was.
  • 20% of + plural noun → plural verb (20% of adults were); + uncountable → singular (20% of the land was).
  • Per cent after a number; percentage as a noun without a number. Not 30 percentage.

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
Count of people / thingsthe number of + singular verbThe number of cyclists has doubled.
Uncountable quantitythe amount ofThe amount of waste fell.
A sharethe proportion / percentage ofThe proportion of graduates was highest in Korea.
A figurenumber + per centJust over 40 per cent of homes had internet access.
Right and wrong

✗ The number of tourists were higher in summer.

✓ The number of tourists was higher in summer.

The head noun is number, which is singular.

✗ The amount of cars increased.

✓ The number of cars increased.

Cars are countable: number, not amount.

✗ Around 30 percentage of people walked to work.

✓ Around 30 per cent of people walked to work.

After a number, write per cent (or %). Percentage is the general noun.

Band 7 → band 9

BAND 7 The percent of people who use internet are increasing.

BAND 9 The percentage of households with internet access rose steadily, reaching almost 90% by 2020.

Correct noun (percentage), correct agreement (rose, head = percentage) and a precise figure.

Check yourself

1The number of international students ______ grown every year since 2010.
Show answer

has

Why
Head noun number is singular.

2A large number of respondents ______ unhappy with the service.
Show answer

were

Why
A number of means several, so the verb is plural.

3The (amount / number) of electricity generated from wind tripled.
Show answer

amount

Why
Electricity is uncountable: amount.

4Tap the part that is wrong.
Show answer

Wrong: were

✓ Around 20% of the land was used for farming in 1950.

Why
20% of + an uncountable noun (land) takes a singular verb. With a plural noun it would be plural: 20% of farms were.

Remember

The number of … is. A number of … are. Countable → number; uncountable → amount. Number + per cent.

UNIT 7Task 1 · numbers in phrases
Numbers inside phrases, and approximation
The rule
  • A number + noun used before another noun is an adjective, so no plural -s: a five-year period, a 20-percent rise, a two-hour journey.
  • Hyphenate it: a ten-year plan. Without a following noun, use the plural: over five years.
  • Approximation goes before the number: just over, just under, approximately, roughly, nearly, around, well over.
  • Fractions and shares: a third of, nearly half of, the majority of, a small minority of.
  • Large numbers: 2 million (no s), but millions of people.

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
Duration as an adjectivenumber-noun (singular)over a ten-year period
Rounding a figureapproximation word + numberjust under 60%
Describing a sharefraction / majorityRoughly two-thirds of students
Big figuresnumber + million (no s)3.5 million visitors
Right and wrong

✗ over a five years period

✓ over a five-year period

Number + noun before another noun: singular, hyphenated.

✗ The population reached 8 millions.

✓ The population reached 8 million.

After a number, million has no -s.

✗ Almost the half of the workers were women.

✓ Almost half of the workers were women.

Half takes no article in this pattern.

Band 7 → band 9

BAND 7 It was about 49.7% in the first year and it was 70.2% at the end.

BAND 9 The figure began at just under half and had risen to well over two-thirds by the end of the period.

Band 9 rounds sensibly and turns figures into fractions, so the trend is easier to read. Exact figures are still fine for the key points.

Check yourself

1The survey covered a ______ period.
Show answer

three-month

Why
Number + noun before a noun: singular and hyphenated.

2In 2010, ______ people used public transport daily.
Show answer

2 million

Why
Number + million, no -s and no of.

3At 51%, (most / the majority) of the votes went to the Green Party.
Show answer

the majorityalso accepted: most

Why
Both work. The majority of sounds more formal; most of is also correct.

4Tap the part that is wrong.
Show answer

Wrong: twenty-years

✓ The government launched a twenty-year plan to reduce emissions.

Why
Inside a noun phrase, the number + noun is singular: a twenty-year plan.

Remember

a five-year period, 8 million, almost half of. Put the approximation word before the number.

UNIT 8Task 1 · articles with data
Articles in chart descriptions
The rule
  • the before a specific measure: the number of, the proportion of, the figure for, the highest.
  • Countries: no article (Japan, France) except plural or political names: the UK, the USA, the Netherlands.
  • Decades take the: in the 1990s. Single years do not: in 1995.
  • General plural or uncountable nouns: no article: Cars were the main form of transport.
  • a / an for one of something: a sharp rise, an 8% fall (an because eight starts with a vowel sound).

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
A measurethe + noun + ofThe proportion of women rose.
A countryzero article (or the for UK/USA)The UK spent more than Italy.
A timein + year / in the + decadein 2001 · in the 2000s
A single changea / anan 11% drop
Right and wrong

✗ Number of students rose.

✓ The number of students rose.

A specific measure takes the.

✗ in 1980s

✓ in the 1980s

Decades take the.

✗ There was a 8% decline.

✓ There was an 8% decline.

an before a vowel sound: an eight…

Four missing articles restored. No new vocabulary needed: accuracy alone moves this sentence up a band.

Decades take the; single years do not.

Show answer

Wrong: proportion

UNIT 9Task 2 · general ideas
Articles when you talk about things in general
The rule

Essays talk about things in general, and that is where most article errors happen.

  • General plural: no article. Children need routine. Cars pollute cities.
  • General uncountable: no article. Education, technology, society, nature, crime, happiness. Not the society.
  • the for one specific or unique thing: the internet, the environment, the government (of a country), the economy.
  • the when the noun is limited by a phrase after it: the education of girls in rural areas.
  • A singular countable noun can never stand alone: a car, the car, cars. Not car is useful.

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
General idea, pluralno articleTeenagers are influenced by advertising.
Unique / shared thingtheThe internet has changed the economy.
Limited by an of phrasethethe cost of living in cities
One examplea / anA child who reads daily…
Right and wrong

✗ The society should respect the elderly people.

✓ Society should respect elderly people.

General ideas: no article. (The elderly as a group noun is also correct.)

✗ Internet has made life easier.

✓ The internet has made life easier.

The internet is unique: always the.

✗ Government should invest in public transport.

✓ The government (or Governments) should invest in public transport.

Government as a body is countable: the government or governments.

Band 7 → band 9

BAND 7 The technology is very important in the modern life of the people.

BAND 9 Technology now shapes almost every aspect of modern life.

Three unnecessary articles removed and the idea made concrete. Correct articles make a script feel native-like.

Check yourself

1______ is one of the main causes of stress among young people.
Show answer

Unemployment

Why
A general uncountable idea: no article.

2Many people now buy everything on ______ .
Show answer

the internet

Why
The internet always takes the.

3 (the / —) quality of the air in big cities is getting worse.
Show answer

the

Why
Quality is limited by of the air in big cities, so it takes the.

4Tap the part that is wrong.
Show answer

Wrong: the nature

✓ In my opinion, nature should be protected at all costs.

Why
Nature in general takes no article.

Remember

General plural or uncountable: no article. Unique or limited by of…: the. Never a bare singular countable.

UNIT 10Task 2 + Task 1
Countable or uncountable?
The rule

Some nouns that are countable in other languages are uncountable in English. They take no a, no plural -s and a singular verb:

information, advice, research, evidence, knowledge, equipment, furniture, traffic, homework, progress, feedback, pollution, transport, accommodation, software, luggage

  • Count them with a unit: a piece of advice, a piece of research, a study, two items of equipment.
  • much / little / less / an amount of + uncountable. many / few / fewer / a number of + countable.
  • a lot of / plenty of / more / most / some work with both.

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
Supporting an argumentresearch / evidence (uncountable)Research shows that…
Counting studiesa study / studiesSeveral studies have found…
Quantitiesmuch / little + uncountableLittle progress has been made.
Numbers of thingsmany / fewer + countableFewer jobs are available.
Right and wrong

✗ Many researches show that…

✓ Much research shows (or Many studies show) that…

Research is uncountable.

✗ She gave me an advice.

✓ She gave me some advice / a piece of advice.

Advice is uncountable.

✗ These informations are useful.

✓ This information is useful.

Uncountable: singular verb and this.

Band 7 → band 9

BAND 7 There are many evidences that the traffics is causing a lot of pollutions.

BAND 9 There is considerable evidence that traffic is a major source of pollution.

Every uncountable noun is now used correctly. One sentence free of three errors moves accuracy up a band.

Check yourself

1Students need more ______ about career choices.
Show answer

information

Why
Information is uncountable.

2There is ______ evidence to support this claim.
Show answer

little

Why
Evidence is uncountable: little.

3The equipment in many schools (be) out of date.
Show answer

is

Why
Equipment is uncountable: singular verb.

4Tap the part that is wrong.
Show answer

Wrong: researches

✓ Recent research suggests that sleep affects memory. (Or: Recent studies suggest…)

Why
Research has no plural. Use research suggests or studies suggest.

Remember

information, advice, research, evidence, equipment, knowledge, traffic, progress: no a, no -s, singular verb.

UNIT 11Task 2 + Speaking
Subject–verb agreement in long sentences
The rule

The verb agrees with the head of the subject, not with the noun nearest to it. Find the head by removing the phrases after it.

  • The use of mobile phones in schools is … (head: use)
  • One of the main reasons is … (head: one)
  • Everyone, each, every, nobody, somebody → singular: Everyone has.
  • There is + singular / uncountable; there are + plural. There are many reasons why…
  • Two subjects joined by and → plural: Diet and exercise are both important.

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
Long noun phrase subjectverb agrees with the first nounThe number of hours children spend online is worrying.
One ofsingular verbOne of the biggest problems is cost.
There + pluralthere areThere are several solutions.
Gerund subjectsingular verbLearning languages is rewarding.
Right and wrong

✗ One of the reason are cost.

✓ One of the reasons is cost.

One of the + plural noun + singular verb.

✗ The quality of public hospitals are declining.

✓ The quality of public hospitals is declining.

Head noun: quality.

✗ Everybody have a right to education.

✓ Everybody has a right to education.

How long → I've been -ing. Childhood → used to. Plans → I'm going / hoping to. Wishes → I wish I could / had.

UNIT 24Speaking + Writing
Word order: adverbs, enough, and indirect questions
The rule
  • Frequency adverbs go before the main verb and after be: I always walk. I am always late.
  • also follows the same rule: It also saves time. It is also cheap.
  • enough goes after an adjective but before a noun: old enough · enough money.
  • Indirect questions use statement order: I don't know what the answer is. Not what is the answer.
  • Do not put an adverb between a verb and its object: I like very much football ✗ → I like football very much.

Where it fits

SituationUseExample
Speaking: habitsadverb before the verbI usually cook at home.
Speaking: unsure answerstatement order after I'm not sure what…I'm not sure why people do that.
Essay: adding a pointalso before the verbIt also creates jobs.
Essay: amountenough + nounThere is not enough housing.
Right and wrong

✗ I go always to the gym on Fridays.

✓ I always go to the gym on Fridays.

Frequency adverb before the main verb.

✗ I don't know what is the reason.

✓ I don't know what the reason is.

Indirect question: subject before verb.

✗ Salaries are not enough high.

✓ Salaries are not high enough.

Enough after an adjective.

Band 7 → band 9

BAND 7 I like very much travelling and I go often abroad.

BAND 9 I really enjoy travelling, and I try to go abroad at least once a year.

Adverbs placed correctly and a precise frequency phrase instead of often.
